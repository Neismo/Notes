
# Probabilistic Machine Learning
Theoretical concepts of probabilistic machine learning. Abstract concepts, but used widely. 

First recall:
$$\begin{align}
\text{Product Rule} &\quad \text{Sum Rule} &\quad \text{Conditional} &\quad \text{Conditional Independence} \\
p(a,b)=p(b|a)p(a) &\quad p(b)=\int_a p(a,b) &\quad p(a|b)=\frac{p(a,b)}{p(b)} &\quad p(a,b|c) = p(a|c)p(b|c) \\
\end{align}$$

- A probabilistic model is *==completely==* specified by its *joint distribution*
- Consider a model with *two random variables*: $y$ ( observed data) and $\theta$ (unknown parameter)
- The *join distributution* can be expressed with the *product rule*:
$$ p(y,\theta) = p(y|\theta)p(\theta) $$p(y|\alpha,\beta,x)p(\alpha,\beta|x)p(y|\alpha,\beta,x)p(\alpha,\beta|x)
- The *posterior distribution* can be obtained by *conditioning* on $y$:
$$ p(\theta|y) = \frac{p(y,\theta)}{p(y)} = \underbrace{\frac{p(y|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)}}_{\text{Bayes' Rule}} $$
- The *evidence* term can be derived from the *joint distribution* via the *sum rule*:
$$ p(y) = \int_\theta p(y,\theta) = \int_\theta p(y|\theta)p(\theta) $$
## More observed variables than 1:
What if we observe more than one, example $y_1,y_2$? For a broad class of probabilistic models, the likelihood term can be *decomposed* using *conditional independence*:
$$ p(y_1,y_2|\theta) = p(y_1|\theta)p(y_2|\theta) $$
or generally for $\mathbf{y} = [ y_1, \cdots y_n ]$:
$$ p(y_1,\cdots y_N|\theta) = \prod_{n=1}^N p(y_n|\theta) $$

The *joint distribution* is then:
$$ p(\mathbf{y},\theta) = p(\mathbf{y}|\theta)p(\theta) =\prod_{n=1}^N p(y_n|\theta)p(\theta)$$
## A probabilistic perspective on making predictions
>[!note] General guide
>Given some data $y$, what can we say about a new observation $y^*$
> 1) Formulate *joint distribution* for *all variables* of interests
> $$ p(y^*,y,\theta) = p(y^*,y|\theta)p(\theta) = \underbrace{p(y^*|\theta)p(y|\theta)p(\theta)}_{\text{Cond. Independence}} $$
> 2) Condition on *observed data* $y$:
> $$ p(y^*,\theta|y) = \frac{p(y^*,y,\theta)}{p(y)} = \frac{p(y^*|\theta)p(y|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)} $$
> 3) *Marginialize* out $\theta$ using *sum rule* to get *predictive posterior distribution*
> $$ p(y^*|y) = \int_\theta p(y^*,\theta|y) = \int_\theta \frac{p(y^*|\theta)p(y|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)} = \int_\theta p(y^*|\theta)p(\theta|y) = \mathbb{E}_{p(\theta|y)}[p(y^*|\theta)]$$
> >[!note]- Last Step Explanation
> > In the next-to-last step, *Bayes'* was used on $\frac{p(y|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)} = p(\theta|y)$!
^predictiveposterior

For the Beta-binomial model, this will result in the following:
$$ p(y^* = k|y) = \binom{N^*}{k}\frac{B(\alpha+k,\beta+N^*-k)}{B(\alpha,\beta)} $$

# The Plug-in Approximation

*Recall* the ==gaussian distributions==:
$$\mathcal{N|\mu,\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma}},\quad \mu=\mathbb{E}[X],\quad \sigma^2=\mathbb{V}[X]$$

## Dirac's Delta Distribution
Assume a normal distribution $x\sim \mathcal{N|\mu,\sigma^2}$, and $\lim \sigma^2\rightarrow 0$. Then $x$ behaves as a *constant* around $\mu.$ We say that $x$ follows a ==Dirac's Delta Distribution== centered at $\mu$:
$$p(x)=\lim_{\sigma^2\rightarrow0}\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\sigma^2)=\delta(x-\mu)$$
Important properties:
$$\begin{align}
\delta(x-\mu) &= \left\{\begin{matrix} \infty & \text{if }x=\mu \\ 0 & \text{otheriwse} \end{matrix}\right. \\
\int_x\delta(x-\mu) &= 1 \\
\int_xf(x)\delta(x-\mu) &= f(\mu)
\end{align}$$
The last property is called the **sifting property** and implies:
$$\mathbb{E}_{\delta(x-\mu)}[f(x)] = f(\mu)$$

## Plug-in Approximation
From the last section, we found the *optimal* model as:
$$ p(y^*|y)  = \int_\theta p(y^*|\theta)p(\theta|y) $$
but it *can be* non-trivial to compute. Assume a *best parameter* $\hat\theta$ exists (like a *maximum likelihood estimate*), then we can approximate $p(\theta|y)$ using a **Dirac's delta function**:
$$ p(\theta|y)\approx \delta(\theta-\hat\theta) $$
and applying the *shifting property* from that distribution yields:
$$ p(y^*|y) = \int_\theta p(y^*|\theta)p(\theta|y) \approx \int_\theta p(y^*|\theta)\delta(\theta-\hat\theta) = p(y^*|\hat\theta)$$
and is the reason it is called the **plug-in approximation**. This, however, *ignores* any uncertainties, and produce *overconfident* predictions

# Grid Approximations
We also wan't to deal with *non-conjugate* models, unlike the *Beta-Binomial* model we saw earlier. We will learn how to compute and approximate these *intractable distributions*:
1) Grid Approximation
2) Laplace Approximation
3) Variational Inference
4) Markov Chain Monte Carlo methods (MCMC)

Goals for these methods is to ==copmpute *tractable approximation*== $q(\theta)$ of true posterior $p(\theta|y)$ such that:
- $q(\theta)$ resembles the true posterior $p(\theta|y)\approx q(\theta)$
- $q(\theta)$ should be *tractable* such that we can compute posterior summaries, predictions, etc.

>[!note]+ Construction for a Beta-Binomial Model
>Construction of the grid approximation for the known beta-binomial model gives us insights, despite the fact that we know its analytical form. We know that $\theta\in[0;1]$, so we can define:
>- Grid points for $\theta:0\leq\theta_1<\theta_2<\cdots<\theta_M\leq 1$
>- Evaluate the **exact posterior** (up to a constant) at all the grid points:
>$$ \tilde\pi_i \propto p(\theta_i|y)\propto p(y|\theta_i)p(\theta_i) $$
>- Sum all the values to get normalization constant $Z=\sum_{i=1}^M\tilde\pi_i$
>- Compute normalized probabilities $\pi_i=\frac{1}{Z}\tilde\pi_i$ to get the grid approximation:
>$$ q(\theta) = \sum_{i=i}^M\pi_i\delta(\theta-\theta_i) $$

*Caveats*:
- Choosing in a small interval is easy, but what if we are to choose $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$? Then we need to define our interval $[a,b]$. Often identified *visually*
- Scales *poorly* with dimensionality. Do not scale well beyond 3 or 4 dimensions
- Number of grid points is a balancing task between computation cost and accuracy. Diminishing returns.
