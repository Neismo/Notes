# Prior Distributions on Function Spaces

Parametric models usually follow these 3 steps:
1) Specify prior $p(\mathbf{w})$ and likelihood $p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w})$.
2) Calculate posterior distribution $p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y})$
3) Make predictions based on on the predictive posterior distribution $p(y^*|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^*)$

That is to say, once we have calculated the posterior distribution, then we no longer *need the data!* Linear and logistic regression are example models of *parametric models*, models controlled by a finite-dimensional parameter vector.

Recall our linear model:
$$ y_{n} = f_{n} + e_{n} = \phi(\mathbf{x}_{n})^\top\mathbf{w}+e_{n}, $$
where the goal was to learn the latent function parameters $\mathbf{w}$ in $f_{n}=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{n})^\top\mathbf{w}$. Before we focused on the *join distribution* $p(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y})$, but now, let us denote $\mathbf{f}=[f(\phi(x_{1}))\quad f(\phi(x_{2}))\cdots\quad f(\phi(x_{N}))]$. We not model the *join distribution as*:
$$ p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{f}) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w}), $$
which can be become the original if we marginalize out $\mathbf{f}$:

$$ \begin{align}
 p(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}) = \int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w}) \partial \mathbf{f}.
\end{align}$$

If we now instead marginalize out $\mathbf{w}$, we get:
$$ \begin{align}
p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{f}) &= p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})\int p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w})\partial \mathbf{w} \\
&= p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}),\text{ where }p(\mathbf{f})=\int p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w})\partial \mathbf{w}
\end{align} $$

If we continue with a ==Gaussian prior on **w**==, $\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0,\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}})$, then the distribution of $p(\mathbf{f})$ will be a Gaussian again due to the [Gaussian properties](3.%20Bayesian%20Linear%20Regression#Recap%20Multivariate%20Gaussian%20Distribution), and as $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{\Phi w}$, we have the nice form of:
$$\mathbf{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Phi}\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I\Phi}^\top) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{K}).$$

The covariance between two function realizations $f_{i},f_{j}$ is
$$ \begin{align}
\mathbf{K}_{ij} &= \text{cov}(y_{i},y_{j})  \\
&= \text{cov}(f(\mathbf{x}_{i}),f(\mathbf{x}_{j})) \\
&= \text{cov}(\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\mathbf{w},\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j})^\top\mathbf{w}) \\
&= \phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\text{cov}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w})\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j}) \\
&= \phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{w}]\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j}) \\
&= \phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j}) \\
&= \frac{1}{\alpha}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j}) \equiv k(x_{i},x_{j}).
\end{align} $$
Different covariance functions can give rise to different realizations. Here are some examples, with samples from the the Gaussian with said covariance matrices.

![[GPVarianceFunctions.png]]

As we can see, the *Squared Exponential* gives rise to non-linear samples.

>[!note] Summary So Far
>1) We started with a **Bayesian Linear Model**
>$$ p(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w}) $$
>2) We introduced **f** into the model and marginalized over the weights **w**
>$$ p(\mathbf{y,\mathbf{f}}) = \int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w}) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}) $$
>3) This gave us a **prior for linear functions** in functions space $p(\mathbf{f})$, where the covariance function for $\mathbf{f}$ was given by
>$$ k(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}_{j}) $$
>4) However, **changing the covariance function**, we can model ==much more interesting functions!==

# Gaussian Process Regression

>[!faq] Definition of a Gaussian Process
>A **Gaussian Process** (GP) is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have a *joint Gaussian distribution*!
>$$ f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')), $$
>It is ==completely characterized== by the mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ and by its covariance function $k(\cdot,\cdot).$ Not *all covariance functions* are valid.

Remember, in *linear systems*, we had that the **Gaussian** is a conjugate to *itself*, meaning the **posterior is also Gaussian**.
$$ p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{Wz}+\mathbf{b},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{y})\quad\quad p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{\mu}_{z},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z}). $$

The *join distribution* $p(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}\\\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix}|\mathbf{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)$ in closed form with:
$$ \mathbf{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{\mu}_{z} \\ \mathbf{W\mu}_{z}+\mathbf{b}
\end{bmatrix}, \mathbf{\Sigma} = \begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z} & \mathbf{\Sigma}_{z}\mathbf{W}^\top \\ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z} & \mathbf{\Sigma}_{y} + \mathbf{W\Sigma}_{z}\mathbf{W}^\top
\end{bmatrix}, $$

The *posterior* also has a closed form, and is
$$ \begin{align}
p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{\mu}_{z|y},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z|y}) \\
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z|y}^{-1} &= \Sigma_{z}^{-1}+\mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{\Sigma}_{y}\mathbf{W} \\
\mu_{z|y} &= \mathbf{\Sigma}_{z|y}\left[ \mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{\Sigma}_{y}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z}^{-1}\mathbf{\mu}_{z} \right]
\end{align} $$
as well as the *marginal*
$$ p(\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{W\mu}_{z}+\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{\Sigma}_{y}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{z}\mathbf{W}^\top). $$
^marginal

Our new model is now 
$$ y_{n} = f(\mathbf{x}_{n}) + e_{n} $$

The likelihood for all points are 
$$ p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}) = \prod_{n=1}^N\mathcal{N}(y_{n}|f_{n},\beta^{-1})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f},\beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}), $$
where we impose a prior on the function values $\mathbf{f} = [f(\mathbf{x}_{1})\cdots f(\mathbf{x}_{N})]$:
$$ p(\mathbf{f}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{K}), \text{ for } \mathbf{K}_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}) $$
**Goal**: compute predictive posterior distribution for $y^*$ so $p(y^*|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^*)$. This is a ==two step process==:
1) Calculate the joint distribution $p(\mathbf{y},y^*|\mathbf{x}^*)$
2) Use the rule of conditioning in Gaussian distributions to compute $p(y^*|\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^*)$.

## Computations
$$ \begin{align} 
p(\mathbf{f}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{K}) \\
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{f},\beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}).\end{align} $$

We showed [here](5.%20Gaussian%20Process%20Regression#^marginal) that the marginal in such a system is computed as, and with above system we get
$$ p(\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{\mathbf{0},\beta^{-1}\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{K}}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{C}), $$
with $\mathbf{C}=\beta^{-1}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{K}$.

If we introduce a single new data point, then we can write $\tilde{y}=[y(\mathbf{x}^*),\mathbf{y}]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N+1}$, and utilizing the conditioning for Gaussian shown above:
$$ p(\mathbf{\tilde{y}})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\tilde{C}}),\text{ for } \mathbf{\tilde{C}} = \begin{bmatrix}
c & \mathbf{k} \\ \mathbf{k}^\top & \mathbf{C}
\end{bmatrix}. $$
where
$$\begin{align}
c &= k(\mathbf{x}^*, \mathbf{x}*) + \beta^{-1}, \\
\mathbf{k} &= [k(\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{x}_{1}),\cdots, k(\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{x}_{N})]
\end{align}$$
and we can now calculate:
$$ p(y^*|\mathbf{y}) = \mathcal{N}(y^*|\mu_{y^*|\mathbf{y}},\sigma^2_{y^*|\mathbf{y}}) = \mathcal{N}(y^*|\mathbf{kC}^{-1}\mathbf{y}, c-\mathbf{kC}^{-1}\mathbf{k}^\top). $$

## Intuition about Gaussian Process

Similar input should have similar output: $\mathbf{x} \approx \mathbf{x}'\rightarrow f(\mathbf{x})\approx \mathbf{x'}$.

We require to specify a covariance function, of which the *squared exponential* is popular: $\text{cov}(f(\mathbf{x}),f(\mathbf{x'})) = k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x'})=\exp\left( -\frac{||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x'}||^2}{2} \right),$
and we can see that the covariance is calculated based on the inputs!

# Covariance Functions

A ==covariance function== $k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, maps a pair of inputs to the real line. This lets us form the covariance:
$$ \mathbf{K}_{ij} = \text{cov}(f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})) = k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}) $$

We require that they are ==symmetric== and ==Positive Semi-definite== (PSD):
$$ \begin{align}
\mathbf{K} &= \mathbf{K}^\top\tag{Symmetric} \\
\forall \mathbf{x}\neq 0&: \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}\geq 0\tag{PSD} 
\end{align} $$

Functions as *prior information*.

## Squared Exponential Kernel
Focus primarily on the ==squared exponential kernel==
$$ k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \kappa^2\exp\left( -\frac{||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'||_{2}^2}{2\ell^2} \right), $$
where $\kappa$ controls the **magnitude** and $\ell$ is called the **lengthscale**. As you can see below, $\kappa$ controls the magnitudes we can see - it is the *noise variance* - while the lengthscale $\ell$ controls the *smoothness*
