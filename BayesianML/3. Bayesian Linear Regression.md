
# Recap Multivariate Gaussian Distribution
Gaussian behave nicely:
- Closed under *affine transformations*: $x\sim\mathcal{N}(m,b)$, then $a+bx\sim\mathcal{N}(a+bm,b^2v)$
- Two independent Gaussian also add up to a Gaussian:
$$ x\sim\mathcal{N}(m_x,b_x),\quad y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_y,b_y),\quad x+y\sim\mathcal{N}(m_x+m_y,v_x+v_y)$$
Same for *multivariate Gaussian*!

$$ \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{Bx} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{Bm},\mathbf{BVB^\top}),\text{ where } \mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{V}) $$
^gaussianprops

Functional form of the ==univariate Gaussian distribution== (in log):
$$ \ln\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\sigma^2) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}x^2+\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}x+K,$$
For some constant $K$. That means if we can identify a log-distribution on this form, it is a *univariate normal distribution!*

For a ==multivariate Gaussian Distribution==, with mean $\mathbf{\mu}=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]$ covariance $\mathbf{\Sigma} = \text{Cov}[\mathbf{x}]$, it has functional form:

$$ \ln p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{\mu}^\top\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} + K$$
^functionalform
# Bayesian Linear Regressions

A simplified setup: assume $\sigma^2$ is known and fixed. Then Bayes' states:
$$ p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w})}{p(\mathbf{y})} $$
The likelihood we already know is Multivariate Gaussian:
$$ p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w},\sigma^2) = \prod_{n=1}^N\mathcal{N}(y_n|\mathbf{w}^\top\phi(x_n),\sigma^2) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}),$$
Where $\Phi$ is the collection of basis functions $\phi_n(\mathbf{x})$!

The prior will follow a *conjugate* for $\mathbf{x}$, a Gaussian, which implies our *posterior will be of the same shape*:
$$ p(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) $$

## The MAP estimator
Looking back at the posterior with this we have
$$  p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w})}{p(\mathbf{y})} = p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) = \frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})}{p(\mathbf{y})}  $$

The ==Maximum A Posteori== estimator (MAP) is $\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\mathbf{w} p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y})$. The denominator above does not depend on $\mathbf{w}$, so we can continue and say:
$$\begin{align}
p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) &\propto \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \\
\ln p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) &\propto \ln\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I}) + \ln\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \\
&= \underbrace{\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)}_{\text{Constant w.r.t w}}-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-\mathbf{w}^\top\phi(x_n))^2 - \underbrace{\frac{D}{2}\ln(2\pi\alpha^{-1})}_{\text{Constant w.r.t w}} - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} \\
&= -\frac{\beta}{2}\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-\mathbf{w}^\top\phi(x_n))^2}_{\text{MSE}} - \frac{\alpha}{2}\underbrace{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w}}_{\text{Ridge}} + K \\
\end{align}$$

In the end, we have a ==MAP estimator== equivalent to *ridge regression* where $\lambda=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}$ and we get a *maximum likelihood estimation* if $\alpha\rightarrow 0$.

### Deriving the posterior distribution of weights

With the function form seen [here](#^functionalform) we try from the previous slide to see if we get it to that form:
$$\begin{align}
\ln p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) &= -\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-\mathbf{w}^\top\phi(x_n))^2 - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} + K \\
&= -\frac{\beta}{2}(\mathbf{y}-\Phi\mathbf{w})^\top(\mathbf{y}-\Phi\mathbf{w}) - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} + K \\
&= -\frac{\beta}{2}(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}-2\mathbf{y}^\top\Phi\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{w}\Phi^\top\Phi\mathbf{w}) - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} + K \\
&= -\frac{\beta}{2}(-2\mathbf{y}^\top\Phi\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{w}\Phi^\top\Phi\mathbf{w}) - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} + K \\
&= -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top(\beta\Phi^\top\Phi+\alpha\mathbf{I})\mathbf{w}+\beta\mathbf{y}^\top\Phi\mathbf{w}+K
\end{align}$$

Where we can see that:
$$ \mathbf{S}^{-1} = (\beta\Phi^\top\Phi+\alpha\mathbf{I})\leftrightarrow \mathbf{S}=(\beta\Phi^\top\Phi+\alpha\mathbf{I})^{-1}, $$
and
$$ \mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \beta\mathbf{y}^\top\Phi \leftrightarrow \mathbf{m}=\beta\mathbf{S}\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}$$

## Key Equations for Bayesian Linear Regression
WIth a design matrix $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ and observations $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^N$:
$$\begin{align}
p(\mathbf{w}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \tag{Prior}\\
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}) \tag{Likelihood}\\
p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S}) \tag{Posterior}\\
p(\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}+\alpha^{-1}\Phi\Phi^\top) \tag{Marginal Likelihood}\\
\end{align}$$
Using $\beta = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}$:
$$\begin{align}
\mathbf{m} &= \beta\mathbf{S}\Phi^\top\mathbf{y} \\
\mathbf{S} &= (\alpha\mathbf{I}+\beta\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}
\end{align}$$

With the ==two hyperparameters==:
- $\alpha$: prior precision of the regression weights
- $\beta$: precision of the measurements

# The Posterior Predictive Distribution

Now we want to have the *predictive* posterior distribution 
$$p(\mathbf{y}^*|\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}^*\underbrace{\phi(\mathbf{x}^*)}_{\phi_*},\sigma^2)$$

Recall from [last week](2.%20Grid%20Approximations#^predictiveposterior) (last line), that we can write this join distribution as:
$$\begin{align}
 p(\mathbf{y}^*|\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{w}) &= \int p(y^*|\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{w})p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) \partial \mathbf{w} \\
&= \int\mathcal{N}(y^*|\mathbf{w}^\top\phi_{*},\sigma^2)\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S}) \partial \mathbf{w} \\
&= \mathcal{N}(y^*|\mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{\phi}_{*},\mathbf{\phi}_{*}^\top\mathbf{S}\phi_{*}+\sigma^2)
\end{align}$$
We also recall that in this sense, with two Gaussian distributions, that the *resulting* distribution is Gaussian, as seen in the last step above! It utilizes the fact of the functional form. Another way to reason about this is the following.

We have our posterior distribution, $p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S})$, and we are interrested in, for new data $\mathbf{x}_{*}$:

$$ \begin{align}
f^* =f(\mathbf{x}_{*}) &= \mathbf{w}^\top\phi_{*} \\
y^* =y(\mathbf{x}_{*}) &= \mathbf{w}^\top\phi_{*}+\epsilon, \epsilon\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)  
\end{align} $$

Then we have that:
$$\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[f^*] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}^\top\phi_{*}] &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}^\top]\phi_{*} = \mathbf{m}^\top\phi_{*} \\
 \mathbb{V}[f^*] = \mathbb{V}[\mathbf{w}^\top\phi_{*}] &= \phi_{*}^\top\mathbb{V}[\mathbf{w^\top}]\phi_{*} = \phi_{*}^\top\mathbf{S}\phi_{*}
\end{align}$$

Similarly for the realizations of $y^*$, only the variance is different with the extra noise term $\sigma^2$!

# Dealing with Hyper-parameters
Key equations we'er dealing with
$$\begin{align}
p(\mathbf{w}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{0},\alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I}) \tag{Prior}\\
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\Phi\mathbf{w},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}),\quad \beta=\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \tag{Likelihood}\\
p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y}) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{m},\mathbf{S}) \tag{Posterior}\\
p(\mathbf{y}) = p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta) &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\mathbf{I}+\alpha^{-1}\Phi\Phi^\top) \tag{Marginal Likelihood}\\
\end{align}$$
A fully *bayesian model* would require we impose priors on $\alpha,\beta$, i.e.:
$$ p(\alpha,\beta|\mathbf{y}) \propto p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta)p(\alpha,\beta),$$
and *integrate* them out:
$$ p(y^*|\mathbf{y}) = \iiint p(y^*|\mathbf{w},\beta)p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y},\alpha,\beta)p(\alpha,\beta|\mathbf{y}) \partial \mathbf{w}\partial \alpha \partial \beta = \mathbb{E}_{p(\alpha,\beta|\mathbf{y})}[\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y},\alpha,\beta)}[p(y^*|\mathbf{w},\beta)]],$$
However, this is intractable .. we can *approximate* instead.

## Evidence Approximation

To deal with the bastard that is the triple integral, if $\alpha,\beta$ is sharply peaked around $\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}$, then they can be approximated by [Dirac's delta functions](2.%20Grid%20Approximations#Dirac's%20Delta%20Distribution):
$$ p(y^*|\mathbf{y}) \approx \mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{y},\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})}[p(y^*|\mathbf{w},\hat{\beta})] $$
Assume now also we impose *flat priors* on $\alpha,\beta$, then
$$ p(\alpha,\beta|\mathbf{y}) \propto p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta)p(\alpha,\beta)\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta) $$
from which $\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}$ can be estimated by optimizing the *marginal likelihood* $p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta)$:
$$ \hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta}=\arg\max_{\alpha,\beta}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\alpha,\beta)$$
